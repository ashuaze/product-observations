### 1/5

1/5 Free-tier limits can block work before it starts—or halfway through it. Meeting time caps choose the second: everyone can join, then the product can end the shared task. That makes the upgrade signal unusually visible, and unusually risky.

### 2/5

2/5 Zoom Basic limits almost all hosted meetings to 40 minutes, including calls with one host and one participant. Pro extends meetings to 30 hours.

The paid boundary is not “can we meet?” but “can we keep going?”

https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0067966

### 3/5

3/5 On a computer, a Google Meet host without a subscription can run one-on-one meetings for up to 24 hours. With 3 or more participants, the limit is 60 minutes; everyone gets a warning at minute 50.

https://support.google.com/meet/answer/7317473?hl=en

### 4/5

4/5 Both free tiers allow up to 100 participants. Yet Zoom meters long duration even for two people; Meet leaves 1:1 nearly unconstrained, then starts a 60-minute clock at participant three.

The chosen unit decides which job feels “premium.”

https://www.zoom.com/pricing

### 5/5

5/5 A mid-task paywall is felt by every participant, but only the host controls the entitlement.

Measure host upgrades alongside group restarts, participant drop-off, tool switching and unfinished outcomes. A strong paywall reveals value without breaking the job.
