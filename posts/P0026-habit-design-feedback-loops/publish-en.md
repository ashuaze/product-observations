### 1/6

1/6 Habit design is often treated as a bag of growth tricks. Six cases from this cycle suggest a better model: every feature changes a feedback loop—what triggers action, how easy it is to continue, what gets scored, and who can see it.

### 2/6

2/6 Four levers:
• Friction: autoplay removes a stopping point.
• Cadence: notification batching chooses when cues arrive.
• Score: contribution graphs and activity rings define progress.
• Audience: like counts and read receipts make behavior socially visible.

### 3/6

3/6 More measured behavior is not always a better habit. Disabling Netflix autoplay cut daily viewing and session length, but did not change time between sessions. Removing GitHub’s streak counter reduced streaking; work quality was not measured.
https://doi.org/10.1145/3710928

### 4/6

4/6 Social signals do two jobs: feedback for the actor and evidence for the observer. Read receipts can reduce uncertainty and create reply pressure. Forced reciprocity can feel fair while costing privacy. Ask who sees it, who controls it, and what inference it invites.

### 5/6

5/6 Perfection is not habit. In a 12-week study, missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. Among the modelled cases, time to near-maximum automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days.
https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674

### 6/6

6/6 Before shipping a habit feature, ask:
1. What behavior repeats?
2. What outcome improves?
3. What valid non-action state exists?
4. Who sees the signal?
5. What avoidance behavior will we measure?
If only the proxy rises, the loop may be working against the product.
