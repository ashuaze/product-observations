### 1/6

1/6 A sleep playlist can be useful tonight and a poor guide to tomorrow's music discovery. That suggests a test for recommendation controls: can users correct an inference while keeping the activity they still value?

### 2/6

2/6 On Spotify, hiding a song in a playlist stops it playing there. Excluding a playlist from the taste profile reduces the influence of its past and future listens on recommendations. One changes playback; the other changes what listening implies.

https://support.spotify.com/us/article/exclude-playlists-or-tracks-from-your-taste-profile/

### 3/6

3/6 YouTube says Home recommendations rely mainly on watch history, while Up Next mainly uses the current video. A correction needs testing against both future discovery and the usefulness of related viewing in the current session.

https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16089387?hl=en

### 4/6

4/6 Mozilla's 2022 YouTube study used similarity to rejected videos as a measure of bad recommendations. Its methodology acknowledges that one rejection needn't mean suppressing a whole topic. Similarity is a proxy for what the user actually wants.

https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/research/library/user-controls/report/research-methodology/

### 5/6

5/6 If the whole feed is stale, a reset may fit. If someone rejects a whole topic, broad suppression may fit. The case for a narrow correction depends on what the user still wants to keep. Neither small nor sweeping changes are inherently better.

### 6/6

6/6 Define the unwanted inference and the experience to preserve before testing. Across pages and sessions, recheck intent and measure unwanted returns, useful discoveries and correction effort. Fewer similar items alone can't establish a successful repair.
