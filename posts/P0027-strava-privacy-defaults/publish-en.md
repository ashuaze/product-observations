### 1/6

1/6 A privacy default can reduce what's shown while leaving the audience unchanged.

On Strava, hiding a run's endpoints edits the map. Activity visibility decides who can open the record. A map with hidden endpoints can still have a broad audience.

### 2/6

2/6 For new adult accounts, Strava lists Activities as “Everyone.” Its map guide says the first and last 200m of future maps are hidden after the first upload and next app open.

The audience and the map have separate defaults.

### 3/6

3/6 Cropping lets you share the activity, but times on hidden segments stay off leaderboards. Followers/Only You narrow access and exclude the whole activity from public segment leaderboards.

Each choice gives up a different part of the product.

### 4/6

4/6 A 2022 study inferred protected locations from visible routes, distance metadata and street maps. It shows why redaction needs testing for inference, too.

This is historical evidence, not a measurement of today's Strava.
https://doi.org/10.1145/3548606.3560616

### 5/6

5/6 Changing Strava's default activity audience affects new uploads; old activities keep their settings. “Hide all maps” is also future-only.

A privacy change needs a clear time scope and a separate path for past records.

### 6/6

6/6 My design rule: choose defaults for the job—private logging, sharing with friends, or public competition.

Then test whether users can predict who sees what and which old records stay visible. A completed settings flow alone doesn't establish understanding.
